Some indicators try to tell you where price is going. This one starts from the opposite premise: that a lot of the moves you get stopped out on were never real moves to begin with. Liquidity Trap Radar PRO [Thomas] lives in the Trend category on TradingView, but its name points at a specific idea — the liquidity trap — and that's the lens to judge it through.

**A quick caveat up front:** there's no published documentation I can lean on for this one, so this review is about the concept and how a tool like this fits into a workflow. I'm not going to quote settings, thresholds or numbers I can't verify, and I haven't run it.

## What a "liquidity trap" actually means

If you've traded for any length of time, you've felt it. Price pushes past an obvious high or low — the kind of level where stops tend to cluster — then snaps back the other way. The break looked like a breakout. It was a liquidity grab. Someone needed counterparties, and the crowd's stop orders were the counterparties.

A tool branded as a liquidity trap radar is built around detecting that pattern: the sweep, the failure to hold, the reversal back into the prior range. It's a market-structure read, not a momentum read. That's the honest framing, and it's the right way to approach it.

## Where it fits in the trend category

TradingView files this under Trend, which is a slightly awkward home for a concept that's fundamentally about *failed* trends. The connection: a liquidity trap is often the moment a short-term trend exhausts and a new one begins. Read the trap correctly and you're getting early information about a shift in structure rather than chasing a move that's already extended.

That's the value proposition. Whether the tool delivers on it depends on how cleanly it marks those failure points on your chart — and that's something you need to see for yourself on the instruments you actually trade.

## How I'd approach using it

Since I can't cite documented inputs, here's the generic workflow that makes sense for any trap-detection tool:

1. **Treat its signals as context, not entries.** A trap marker tells you "something failed here." It doesn't tell you to click buy or sell. Pair it with your own confirmation — a structure break, a retest, whatever your process uses.
2. **Watch the levels it highlights against your own.** The most useful thing a radar can do is point at liquidity pools you'd have missed. Compare its marks to the obvious swing highs and lows you already see. If it's catching sweeps you wouldn't have flagged, it's earning its place.
3. **Judge it on fakeouts, not clean trends.** The whole thesis is about traps. If the tool is only useful when price is trending normally, the name is doing heavy lifting. Look at how it behaves in choppy, range-bound conditions — that's the test.
4. **Give it time before you judge.** Structure tools tend to look either brilliant or useless depending on the session you happen to load first. Watch it across a few different market conditions before forming a view.

As shown in the chart above, the visual layer is doing real work here — the whole point of a radar is to make an abstract idea (where the stops are) visible at a glance, and that's the kind of thing that only earns its keep if the marks line up with reality.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**
- Attacks a genuinely under-served problem. Most retail tools chase breakouts; few are built to question them.
- Conceptually sound. Liquidity sweeps and stop hunts are real market mechanics, not indicator mysticism.
- Useful as a *filter*. Even if you never take a signal from it directly, a trap read can stop you from buying into an obvious sweep.
- Sits in a category (Trend) where it can complement rather than duplicate your existing momentum tools.

**Cons**
- The name promises a lot. "Radar" implies detection you can trust — and any pattern-based tool will produce false positives in fast or thin conditions.
- No documentation to work from means the learning curve is on you. You'll have to reverse-engineer what its marks mean through observation.
- Trap logic is inherently contextual. A tool that flags failures needs a reference range to judge failure *against*, and that judgement is where these tools live or die.
- Not a standalone system. If you're looking for a plug-and-play signal generator, this isn't the honest pitch.

## Who it's for

Discretionary traders who already think in terms of market structure and liquidity — the ones who mutter "that was a stop hunt" at their screen. If you use order flow concepts, smart money ideas, or simply trade reversals off swept highs and lows, a trap radar is aimed at you.

It's less suited to pure trend-followers who want to ride momentum and don't care why a move started, and to anyone who wants a single indicator to make decisions for them.

## FAQ

**Does it repaint?**
I can't confirm that from available material. Structure-based tools sometimes redraw as new bars confirm a failed break — test it on a live chart before trusting historical marks.

**Can I use it on any timeframe or market?**
The concept of liquidity traps applies across markets, but trap frequency and noise vary a lot by instrument and timeframe. There's no documented "best" setting, so calibrate to what you trade.

**Is it a buy/sell signal tool?**
Treat it as a context and filtering layer. The strongest use is avoiding bad trades and confirming reversals, not generating entries on its own.

**Does it replace order flow data?**
No. It's a price-based read on where liquidity likely sits. Real order flow gives you the tape; this gives you the map.

Liquidity Trap Radar PRO [Thomas] takes a real market mechanic — the stop-hunt fakeout — and builds a trend-category tool around flagging it. The idea is legitimate, the category placement makes sense, and the practical value of catching sweeps you'd otherwise trade into is genuine.

It loses a star for the usual reasons: the name over-promises, there's no documentation to shorten the learning curve, and any detection tool will throw false positives you have to learn to filter. But as a structure-aware companion to your existing setup — a second opinion on whether that breakout is real — it's a sensible addition for the right kind of trader. Not a magic radar. A useful one, if you do the work.
