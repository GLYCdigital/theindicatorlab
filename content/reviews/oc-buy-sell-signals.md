Some indicators try to do everything. OC Buy/Sell Signals takes a narrower path: it's a trend-following tool that marks buy and sell signals directly on the chart. That's the whole premise, and it's worth being upfront about that before we go further. There is no published documentation I can point to for this one, so this review deliberately stays at the conceptual level. No invented settings, no fabricated win rates.

## What it actually does

The indicator belongs to the trend category. In practice that means it watches price behaviour and fires a visual marker when it believes a directional move is underway — a "buy" when the trend turns up, a "sell" when it turns down. As the chart above shows, the signals appear as discrete labels or arrows rather than as a continuous oscillator line.

That distinction matters. You're not reading a value off a scale and making a judgment call about where the line sits. The indicator has already made the judgment for you and reduced it to a yes/no event. Your job shifts from interpretation to confirmation.

## The core idea

Trend indicators generally work by smoothing price data and watching for a change in the smoothed direction. The value of a signal-based version is that it removes the ambiguity of a raw line crossing a threshold. You get a timestamped event you can act on or ignore.

The trade-off is baked into the design. Any signal-based trend tool is, by definition, reactive. It cannot know the future, so it will confirm a turn after some portion of the move has already happened. That's not a flaw unique to this indicator — it's the nature of trend following. The question is whether the signals are clean enough and consistent enough to be useful in a workflow, and that's something you'll have to judge on your own charts rather than take from a review.

## How I'd suggest using it

Because I can't speak to the exact logic, the honest advice is procedural rather than prescriptive:

- **Use it as a bias filter first.** Before taking trades off the signals, watch how they line up with obvious trend structures on your instrument. Do they fire near swing highs and lows, or lag badly?
- **Combine with structure, not in isolation.** A signal marker tells you the indicator sees a trend. It doesn't tell you where your stop goes. Pair it with support/resistance or recent swing points so risk is defined by price, not by a label.
- **Check it across timeframes.** A trend tool that looks tidy on a higher timeframe may chop you up on a lower one. That's a general truth, but worth confirming here since there's no documentation to lean on.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**
- Clean, event-based output. Buy and sell markers are easier to scan than a squiggly line you have to interpret.
- Sits naturally in the trend category, so it plays well as a directional filter alongside your existing setup.
- Visual simplicity means low cognitive load — you glance, you see, you decide.

**Cons**
- Trend signals are inherently lagging. By the time a turn is confirmed, part of the move is gone. Expect that.
- Without published documentation, you can't verify the underlying logic without digging into the source. That's a real gap for anyone who wants to understand *why* a signal fired.
- Signal-based tools can tempt overtrading in ranging markets, where trend logic tends to produce whipsaws.

## Who it's for

This suits discretionary traders who already have a risk framework and want a second opinion on direction — swing traders and position traders especially, since trend signals tend to align better with slower, structural moves. It's less suited to scalpers or anyone wanting a fully mechanical, documented system they can audit line by line.

If you're newer to trend tools, treat the signals as a starting point for your analysis, not the finish line.
