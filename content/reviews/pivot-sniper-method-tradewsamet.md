Pivot Sniper Method is one of those indicators that tries to make market structure visible rather than bury you in signals. As the name suggests, it is built around pivot points — the swing highs and lows that define how a market moves — and it frames them within a trend context. The idea is straightforward: price does not trend in a straight line forever, it advances, pulls back to a pivot, and continues or reverses. This tool puts that skeleton on your chart.

What follows is an assessment of the concept and the tool's stated purpose. TradingView's description for this particular script is thin, so this review sticks to what a pivot-based trend method actually is and what it can and cannot do for you.

## What it actually does

Pivot-based indicators locate local turning points by comparing a bar to those around it. A pivot high is a bar whose high is greater than the highs on either side; a pivot low is the mirror image. Plot enough of them and you get a map of swing structure — where the market last turned, where support and resistance have formed, and whether those points are stepping higher, lower, or sideways.

Framed as a "trend" tool, the value is in reading that structure as a story: higher highs and higher lows mean an uptrend is intact; a break of a pivot low is the first structural crack. The "sniper" part of the name implies precision — using these levels as decision points rather than spraying entries across every candle.

## Key features

Because the source material does not document specific settings, I will not invent any. What can be said honestly is what a tool of this type typically delivers:

- **Swing level mapping.** Pivot highs and lows are marked on the chart, giving you an objective reference for structure instead of eyeballing it.
- **Trend context.** The indicator is categorised under Trend, meaning its purpose is to help you judge direction and continuation, not to fire standalone buy/sell arrows.
- **Level-based decision making.** Pivots double as support and resistance, which makes them useful for stop placement and for judging whether a pullback is healthy or a reversal.

## How to use it

A sensible workflow is to read structure first and take signals second. As shown in the chart above, the tool overlays pivot levels onto price action; your job is to interpret them.

1. **Establish direction.** Are pivots stepping up, down, or flat? That answers the trend question before you think about entries.
2. **Mark the invalidation level.** The most recent opposing pivot is your structural line in the sand. If price closes beyond it, the trend read is wrong.
3. **Treat pullbacks as the opportunity.** A trend indicator is most useful when price retraces toward a pivot and holds — that is where risk is defined and reward is asymmetric.
4. **Combine with momentum.** Pivot structure tells you *where*; momentum tells you *whether*. Confirmation reduces the false breaks that plague every structural method.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**
- Objective structure beats subjective trendlines. Pivots are calculated, not drawn by hand, which removes a lot of bias.
- Levels are actionable. You get concrete prices for stops and targets rather than vague "the trend is up."
- Lightweight by design. It does not try to be a full trading system, which makes it easy to layer with your own rules.

**Cons**
- Pivot methods are inherently lagging. A pivot is only confirmed after the surrounding bars have printed, so you are always reacting to structure that already exists.
- Choppy, range-bound markets produce whipsaw pivots. Any structure tool struggles there, and this one is no exception.
- Thin documentation means you are partly reverse-engineering the author's intent. That is a real barrier for newer traders.

## Who it is for

This suits discretionary traders who think in terms of market structure — swing traders, intraday traders on higher timeframes, and anyone who already uses support and resistance but wants it formalised. It is less suitable for pure mechanical system traders who need fixed rules and historical statistics to trust a tool.

## FAQ

**Does it give buy and sell signals?**
It is a trend and structure tool. Treat the pivot levels as decision zones and pair them with your own entry triggers.

**Which timeframes work best?**
The source does not specify. Pivot structure is generally cleaner on higher timeframes, but that is a general observation, not a documented claim about this script.

**Can I use it alone?**
You can, but structural tools benefit from a momentum or volume confirmation layer. Relying on pivots alone invites whipsaw.

## Verdict

Pivot Sniper Method does a legitimate job: it makes swing structure explicit and gives you levels to trade against. It is not magic, and the name oversells the "sniper" precision, but the underlying method is sound and the tool is honest about being a trend and structure aid rather than a signal machine. The main knock is the lack of documentation, which leaves some of the author's choices opaque.

For traders who already think in pivots and want a clean visual layer, it earns a solid four stars.
