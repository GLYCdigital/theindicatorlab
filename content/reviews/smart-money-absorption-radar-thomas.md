"Smart money" is one of the most abused phrases in retail trading. Most indicators carrying that label are just a moving-average crossover wearing a suit. The Smart Money Absorption Radar Thomas is filed under the Trend category and built around the idea of absorption — the moment aggressive order flow gets soaked up rather than pushing price further. That's a legitimate market concept. Whether an indicator can reliably surface it on a chart is the real question, and it's the one worth answering honestly.

Because there's no published documentation to work from here, this review sticks to what the tool is *about* rather than inventing knob-by-knob specifics. Treat this as a guide to whether the concept and category fit your trading, not a settings manual.

## What it's actually doing

Absorption is a simple idea dressed in complex language. Sellers hit the bid hard, price doesn't drop — someone with size is absorbing that selling. Buyers lift the offer, price stalls — supply is absorbing demand. In principle, that tells you where a larger participant is positioned, which is why the concept sits naturally in the trend category: absorption at a level often precedes or confirms a directional move.

The indicator's job is to translate that into something visible on a chart. In the broadest terms, you're looking for a tool that flags where price behaviour diverges from the pressure behind it — the signature of absorption. As shown in the chart above, that typically means reading the indicator alongside price rather than trading its output in isolation.

## Where it earns its place

The genuine appeal is conceptual, not cosmetic. Most trend tools tell you *that* a trend exists once it's already moving. An absorption-based approach tries to read intent *before* the move fully commits — catching the handoff from accumulation to markup, or from distribution to markdown. For traders who think in terms of liquidity and order flow rather than lagging averages, that's a meaningfully different lens.

It also slots into the trend category without pretending to be a full system. It's a filter and a confirmation tool, not an entry signal generator. Used that way — as one input among several — it has a clear role.

## How to actually use it

The disciplined workflow is unglamorous:

1. **Read structure first.** Identify the trend or range on your own before the indicator says anything.
2. **Use it as a confirmation layer.** When price reaches a level you already care about, check whether the indicator supports the idea of absorption happening there.
3. **Reject it as a standalone trigger.** Absorption tells you *where* interest sits, not *when* to click buy. Combine it with your own entry logic and risk management.

The traders who get burned by tools like this are the ones who treat the output as a signal to act on blindly. The ones who benefit treat it as context.

## Trade-offs worth knowing

- **It's interpretive.** Absorption is a judgment call even for experienced eyes. Any indicator encoding it will occasionally flag noise as intent, and there's no way around that.
- **No published documentation.** This is a real downside — without a description, you're reverse-engineering behaviour from the chart. That's friction for newcomers.
- **Trend-category overlap.** It won't replace your existing trend tools; it complements them. If you already run a crowded setup, adding another confirmation layer can create analysis paralysis rather than clarity.
- **Concept-heavy.** It rewards traders who already understand order flow. If "absorption" is a new word to you, the indicator won't teach it — you'll need to learn the concept first.

## Who it's for

This is for discretionary traders who already think in terms of supply, demand and liquidity — particularly those trading trend continuation or reversal off key levels. Swing and intraday traders who want a second opinion on whether a level is "real" will get the most out of it. It's a poor fit for mechanical, rules-based systems that need a binary signal, and a poor fit for beginners who haven't yet built the underlying mental model.
