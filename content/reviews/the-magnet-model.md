Some indicators try to explain the market. The Magnet Model falls into the other camp — it tries to organize it. The name tells you most of what you need to know about the concept: price behaves as though it's being pulled toward something, and this tool is built to visualize that pull as a trend-following framework. That's the honest, source-level summary. There's no official documentation to lean on here, so I'm going to keep this review tight and describe the concept rather than pretend I have a spec sheet in front of me.

## What the Concept Actually Is

"Magnet" logic in technical analysis isn't new. The core idea is that certain price levels act as attractors — areas where price tends to gravitate, stall, or resolve. A trend indicator built on this premise is essentially trying to answer one question: is price being pulled upward, downward, or is it drifting without a clear target?

The Magnet Model sits in the Trend category, which matters. It's not a reversal tool. It's not an oscillator. It's designed to give you a directional read and, presumably, some sense of where the move is heading. As shown in the chart above, the visual output is meant to be read as a trend state rather than a signal generator you fire on every bar.

If you've used trend indicators before, you already know the trade-off: the good ones keep you on the right side of a move, and the bad ones whipsaw you into oblivion in chop. Magnet-style logic tends to favor the former, because the whole premise is continuity — price moving toward a target rather than randomly oscillating.

## How You'd Actually Use It

Without documented inputs, I can only describe the workflow in general terms, and I'd rather do that than invent settings that don't exist.

The sensible approach with any trend-following tool like this is:

1. **Establish bias.** Use the indicator to determine whether the prevailing pull is up or down. This is your directional filter.
2. **Wait for alignment.** Don't fight the magnet. If the model says price is being drawn higher, you're looking for longs — not because the indicator told you to buy, but because it told you which way the current is running.
3. **Use it as context, not a trigger.** Trend indicators are notoriously poor at pinpointing entries. Pair this with your own structure, support/resistance, or momentum read for timing.
4. **Respect the flip.** When the trend state changes, that's information. It doesn't have to be an automatic exit, but ignoring it defeats the purpose of running the tool.

If you're the kind of trader who wants a single indicator to tell you when to click buy, this isn't that. If you want a framework for staying aligned with direction, that's the lane this lives in.

## Pros and Cons

**Pros:**

- **Conceptually clean.** The magnet premise is intuitive. You don't need a manual to understand why price moving toward a target matters.
- **Trend-category discipline.** It's not trying to do five jobs. It reads direction. That's a virtue in a sea of kitchen-sink indicators.
- **Works as a filter.** Even without knowing the internals, a directional bias tool has obvious utility for anyone running a system that needs a "trade with the trend or don't trade" rule.

**Cons:**

- **No public documentation.** This is the big one. Without a documented description, you're reverse-engineering behavior from the chart. That's a real barrier for anyone who wants to know what's under the hood before trusting it.
- **Trend tools lag by nature.** Anything that reads direction is going to be late at turns. The magnet concept doesn't change that.
- **Chop is the enemy.** Trend-following frameworks get chopped up in range-bound conditions. Expect that.

## Who It's For

This is for the trader who already has an entry method and needs a directional filter to keep them out of counter-trend trades. Swing traders and position traders working on higher timeframes will likely get more out of it than scalpers, because trend logic needs room to breathe. If you're a discretionary trader who likes a clean visual read on direction, it fits. If you need mechanical rules with documented parameters, the lack of documentation will frustrate you.
