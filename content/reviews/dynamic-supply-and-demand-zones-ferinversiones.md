Supply and demand zones are one of those concepts that sound simple until you try to draw them yourself. Every swing high and low starts to look like a zone, and suddenly your chart is a mess of overlapping rectangles that explain the past beautifully and tell you nothing about the future. Dynamic Supply and Demand Zones [Fer_inversiones] is an attempt to automate that drawing process — to let the script decide where the zones belong so you can focus on the trade decision instead of the markup.

A word up front: the official documentation for this one is thin, so I'm going to describe what the tool is designed to do conceptually rather than pretend I know its exact internals. No invented settings, no made-up win rates. Just an honest read on whether it's worth a slot on your chart.

## What it actually does

The core idea is straightforward. Price reacts at certain levels — areas where aggressive buying or selling previously showed up — and this indicator plots those areas as zones on your chart. "Dynamic" is the operative word here: rather than static horizontal lines you draw once and leave forever, the zones respond to price action, updating as the market evolves.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A hand-drawn zone is a snapshot of your thinking on one particular day. A dynamic zone is meant to reflect the current state of the market. Whether the script does that well is the question every trader has to answer for themselves, but the design intent is sound.

The category tag says "Trend," and that's a reasonable framing — supply and demand zones are fundamentally about where trend continuation or reversal is likely to occur. If price breaks through a demand zone and holds below it, that's trend information. If it bounces, that's confirmation. The zones are the scaffolding; the trend read is the payload.

## The workflow it supports

Here's how a supply-and-demand trader typically uses a tool like this, and where the indicator fits:

**1. Context first.** You still need a directional bias. Zones don't tell you whether to be long or short — they tell you where the interesting decisions happen. Identify the broader trend, then use the zones to time entries within it.

**2. Mark the reaction areas.** The indicator handles the plotting. As shown in the chart above, zones appear as bands where price has previously turned. Your job is to watch how price behaves when it returns.

**3. Trade the reaction, not the touch.** A zone is not a buy button. Price entering a demand zone is the setup; the confirmation — a rejection wick, a shift in momentum, a lower-timeframe structure break — is the trigger. The indicator gives you the location. You supply the timing.

**4. Manage around invalidation.** When price closes decisively beyond a zone, the zone has failed. That's your signal to reassess. The dynamic nature of the tool means zones should reflect this, but you're the one who decides what "decisively" means.

If that workflow sounds like how you already think about markets, this indicator will feel natural. If you're a pure momentum chaser who buys breakouts and never looks back, zones will mostly just clutter your screen.

## Where it earns its keep

The strongest argument for an automated zone tool is **consistency**. Human eyes are inconsistent — you'll draw a zone differently on Tuesday than you did on Monday, depending on your mood and your P&L. A script applies the same logic every time. That removes a real source of error, especially for traders who struggle with discipline around levels.

The second benefit is **speed**. Marking up a chart manually across multiple instruments takes time you could spend on analysis. If the indicator handles the plotting, you can scan more charts in less time.

And the third is **objectivity in review**. When you look back at a trade, an automated zone gives you a fixed reference point. You can ask "did price actually react at the zone?" without arguing with your past self about where the zone should have been.

## The honest trade-offs

Automated zones are only as good as their underlying logic — and since the logic here isn't fully documented, you're partly trusting the author's definition of what constitutes a valid zone. That's not a knock on the script specifically; it's true of every zone indicator. But it means you should spend time watching it on a chart you know well before risking capital on its signals.

There's also a noise problem inherent to the approach. Supply and demand zones proliferate. Price makes a lot of swings, and a script that marks every meaningful reaction will eventually mark a lot of them. You'll need to filter — by timeframe, by confluence with other levels, by your own discretion — or your chart becomes wallpaper.

And one more: zones are descriptive, not predictive. They tell you where reactions *have* happened. They don't promise reactions *will* happen. Treating a zone as a guarantee is how traders get run over.

## Who it's for

This is a tool for **discretionary traders who already think in terms of support, resistance and market structure** and want to automate the tedious part. It suits swing traders and intraday traders on higher timeframes who want a clean, consistent zone map. It's less useful for pure breakout traders, scalpers working on tick charts, or anyone who prefers a single mechanical signal over a framework they have to interpret.

## FAQ

**Does it give buy and sell signals?**
No — at least not in the sense of a mechanical entry arrow. It plots zones. Interpreting price behaviour at those zones is your job.

**Will it work on any timeframe or market?**
Zone concepts apply broadly, but the practical usefulness depends on how much structure the market has. Thin, choppy instruments produce messier zones than liquid ones. That's a general truth about the concept, not a claim about this specific script.

**Do I still need to draw levels myself?**
You can, and many traders will layer their own key levels on top. The indicator doesn't replace your judgment — it saves you time on the repetitive markup.

**Is it repainting?**
I can't verify that from the available documentation, and I won't guess. Check the author's page and watch it live on a demo chart if that matters to you — and it should.

## Verdict

Dynamic Supply and Demand Zones does one job and does it in a way that fits naturally into how supply-and-demand traders already work. It won't hand you a strategy, and the lack of detailed documentation means you'll need to spend some screen time understanding its behaviour before leaning on it. But as a charting aid that enforces consistency and saves markup time, it's a sensible addition for the right kind of trader.

It's not revolutionary — zone indicators are a crowded field — but it's practical, and practicality is underrated.
