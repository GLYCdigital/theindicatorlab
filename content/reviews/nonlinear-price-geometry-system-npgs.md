Some indicators sell you a signal. Others try to sell you a worldview. NPGS leans toward the second camp — its name alone tells you the author is thinking about price as shape and structure rather than a stack of moving averages. Here's an honest read on what you're actually getting.

## What NPGS is, stripped of the branding

It's a **trend study** on TradingView. A study, not a strategy — meaning it plots on your chart and doesn't generate backtest orders or equity curves for you. The framing is "nonlinear price geometry," which is a fancy way of saying it looks at price through a geometric lens rather than a linear one.

That's the honest summary. If you're expecting a full machine-learning pipeline or a neural network with documented architecture, the official description doesn't give you that. What it gives you is a trend tool positioned around the idea that price doesn't move in straight lines, and the indicator is built to reflect that.

## The "nonlinear geometry" pitch — what it actually means for you

Traders get burned by linear tools because markets don't cooperate with straight-line logic. A moving average assumes continuity; price delivers gaps, spikes, and regime flips. An indicator that explicitly brands itself around nonlinearity is at least acknowledging that problem.

The practical takeaway: NPGS is designed to give you a **trend read that accounts for the messiness of real price action** rather than pretending it's a clean sine wave. Whether the underlying math is genuinely nonlinear or just marketed that way, you can't verify from the description alone — and I won't pretend otherwise.

## How you'd actually use it

Since the official description is thin on specifics — no documented default settings, no parameter list, no stated bar windows or thresholds — the workflow is the standard one for a trend study:

- **Attach it to a chart** and read the trend state it displays.
- **Cross-reference with price structure** — higher highs, lower lows, breakouts — rather than treating the indicator as an oracle.
- **Use it as a filter**, not a trigger. Trend studies are best at telling you *which side of the market to be on*, not *when exactly to click*.

If you're the type who needs a fully documented parameter manual before installing anything, you'll be underwhelmed by what's published. If you're comfortable exploring an indicator's behavior on your own charts, that's a non-issue.

## Pros and cons, honestly stated

**Pros**
- The conceptual framing — geometric, nonlinear — is a genuine step away from the over-saturated world of MA-cross clones.
- It's a study, so it plays nicely alongside your existing setup without forcing a strategy framework on you.
- Trend filtering is one of the most useful jobs an indicator can do, and that's the category this tool lives in.

**Cons**
- The published documentation is minimal. You won't find a breakdown of inputs, defaults, or the logic behind the geometry.
- "Nonlinear geometry" is evocative but vague. Without documentation, you're trusting the author's framing rather than verifying it.

## Who should install it

- **Trend followers** who want a second opinion on direction without cluttering the chart with five overlays.
- **Discretionary traders** who like a tool that nudges them toward structure and geometry rather than a mechanical buy/sell arrow.
- **Tinkerers** who enjoy exploring an indicator's behavior empirically rather than reading a manual first.

It's a weaker fit for **systematic traders** who need documented, reproducible parameters, and for anyone who wants a strategy script with performance stats baked in — this isn't that.
