Trend Stress Compass [Harmony Algo] is a TradingView trend study published under the Harmony Algo label. The name tells you most of what the developer is aiming at: it frames trend analysis not just as "up or down" but as a question of stress — the idea being that a trend carries a certain amount of tension, and reading that tension is as useful as reading direction. That framing is the honest hook here. It's also the thing you should scrutinise before you install it.

## What It Actually Does

Strip away the branding and this is a trend-category study — a script that plots on your chart to help you interpret trend conditions. The "Compass" and "Stress" language signals an intent to give you more than a single line or a coloured bar: it wants to orient you (compass) and tell you something about the strain in the move (stress).

Here's the honest caveat up front. The official TradingView listing for this script is essentially a placeholder — an "Essential Account" line and a divider, nothing more. There is no documented parameter list, no methodology write-up, no published thresholds, no explanation of how "stress" is calculated. So anything I tell you about the internal maths would be invention. I'm not going to do that.

What I *can* do is assess the concept, the category, and how a study like this fits into a real workflow — and be straight about the fact that the documentation is thin.

## The Concept, Judged on Its Merits

Trend indicators are a crowded field. The vast majority are variations on the same theme: a moving-average crossover, a slope filter, an ADX-style strength meter, a ribbon that changes colour. They tell you direction and maybe strength. Very few attempt to quantify *stress* — the idea that trends don't fail cleanly, they fail under pressure that builds before the break.

That's a genuinely useful lens. If the script delivers on it, the value is in warning you when a trend is getting strained — overextended, decelerating, or behaving inconsistently with its own recent character — rather than simply flipping a colour and telling you after the fact. As shown in the chart above, the study layers onto your price pane to give that read.

The risk with a concept-first indicator is that the concept becomes the marketing and the implementation becomes a black box. Without documentation, you cannot verify what "stress" means in this specific script. Is it volatility-normalised? Momentum decay? Distance from a mean? You won't know from the listing. That's a real limitation, and it's why this isn't a five-star review.

## How to Approach Using It

Because the inputs aren't documented, the sensible workflow is exploratory rather than prescriptive:

- **Start on a clean chart.** Add the study to a price pane with minimal other clutter so you can see exactly what it plots and how it responds.
- **Watch it across a range of conditions** — a clean directional move, a choppy range, a sharp reversal — and form your own view of what the visually "stressed" state corresponds to.
- **Treat it as a secondary read, not a signal generator.** A stress/compass framing is most useful as context layered on top of your existing entry method, not as a standalone trigger.
- **Read the source if you can.** The script is published as a study; where the developer allows it, inspecting the code is the only way to know what the stress measure actually is.

If you can't inspect the code and the developer hasn't documented it, adjust your expectations accordingly — you're trusting the visual, not a spec sheet.

It's a poor fit for anyone who needs a spec sheet before installing: if you require defined inputs and a written methodology, the current listing won't give you that.
